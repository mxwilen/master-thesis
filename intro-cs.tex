The rise of new technology and the growing number of connected devices has led to an even greater variety of software applications. Adding to this, the vast amount of available development tools and software products helps developers in quickly releasing their projects, faster development cycles and shorter intervals between software releases is a fact \cite{ACM_Current_and_Future_Trends_in_Mobile_Device_Forensics_A_Survey}. While this approach benefits companies in terms of security and reliability, it presents challenges in forensic analysis since investigations into these applications take longer time, and the sheer volume of applications further compounds the problem \cite{android_matching, introductionForensics}.

This thesis further explores this problem and presents a new method for automating version comparison and categorizing relevant changes for use in reverse engineering during forensic investigations.

Applications of digital forensic interest are often analyzed to examine functionality that may affect the results of digital forensic investigations. Therefore, the digital forensic process relies on prior reverse engineering of the application to gain an accurate understanding of the software, ensuring that evidence is correctly extracted, interpreted, and admissible in court. This forensic investigation method is because of this dependent on what type of media and application the digital evidence is to be extracted from. When a new version of an application is released, valuable knowledge about its functionality may no longer hold for reverse engineers \cite{android_matching}. Even minor software updates may change how the information is gathered and which forensic methods should be applied \cite{acm_fingerprinting_survey, nij2004forensic}. As a result, the newly released software version may require manual analysis, a process closely resembling reverse engineering.

Given the rapidly evolving industry in software development and the increasing amount of available applications, the pace needed to keep up with the release cycles becomes challenging. For instance, the open-source end-to-end encrypted messaging application Signal, is a frequent example of an application of forensics interest and has multiple software releases every week \cite{githubSignal}. Other encrypted messaging applications monitored by authorities are EncroChat, Exclu, and Ghost \cite{europaInternationalOperation}, which also have frequent release cycles but are not open-source.

Because of this, conducting a full analysis of every version of all relevant applications would entail an unreasonable amount of work. As described by NFC, without knowledge of the relevant changes between two software releases, forensic work becomes an ad hoc process in which all differences must be investigated, rather than focusing on those that are relevant to the forensic investigation. By categorizing changes and filtering them based on their relevance to the forensic investigation, a decrease in the time needed to analyze a single release of an application could be possible.

\section{Aim}
\label{sec:aim}

This thesis aims to develop a new method for automating version comparison and categorization of relevant changes for use during reverse engineering of software applications in a targeted forensic analysis.

To achieve this, the thesis investigates the development of a tool that can detect programmatic changes between two versions of a software application, categorize these changes, and later filter them based on relevance in the forensic investigation. All without having knowledge of the source code, and only analyzing the released application binaries.

This thesis address the research questions outlined in Section \ref{sec:research-questions} by exploring binary analysis techniques and the tools commonly used for analyzing software executables. Following this, a deep dive into categorizing the discovered changes based on functionality defined as relevant, and what method to perform this is considered the most effective. Ultimately, the research seeks to determine how changes in software applications impact the forensic analysis process and whether these methods can effectively identify significant alterations. 

The primary focus of this thesis lies in producing a method that assesses whether the changes between the two versions' core operations are significant enough to warrant a manual reverse engineering of the application.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}
The thesis does not introduce specific forensic methods or tools utilized during the analysis process. It is instead assumed that the tools are functional and relevant for the core operations of the examined software.
